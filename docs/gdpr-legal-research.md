# GDPR Legal Research: Portfolio Privacy Policy and Terms

Research snapshot: 2026-08-06. Primary-source research, not legal advice. National implementations, controller location, user location, hosting configuration, and actual browser requests still require review.

## Executive findings

- A privacy notice is required when the site processes personal data within GDPR scope. A simple public portfolio can still process personal data through IP addresses, server logs, contact details, analytics identifiers, fonts/CDN requests, and cookie or storage identifiers.
- The notice should identify the controller and contact details, explain purposes and legal bases, categories of data, recipients, transfers, retention or retention criteria, data-subject rights, complaint rights, consent withdrawal, and whether provision is required. Give this information at collection, in concise and accessible language.
- Optional analytics, advertising, behavior measurement, third-party embeds, and non-essential browser storage should be off until valid consent. ePrivacy Directive art. 5(3) governs storing/accessing information on terminal equipment; GDPR governs subsequent personal-data processing. Consent may therefore be needed under both regimes.
- Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous. No pre-ticked boxes or consent inferred from browsing. Withdrawal must be possible at any time and as easy as giving consent. Withdrawal affects future processing; it does not make earlier lawful processing unlawful.
- GDPR does not require Terms of Service merely because a personal portfolio is online. Terms become relevant if the site creates contracts, sells services/products, accepts submissions, licenses content, runs accounts, or promises service terms. Separate statutory website-information duties can still apply, especially for an economic service.

## Privacy notice disclosures

GDPR arts. 12 and 13 require transparent, concise, intelligible, easily accessible information when personal data is collected from the person. Article 13 requires, at minimum:

- controller identity and contact details, plus the data-protection-officer contact where applicable;
- purposes of processing and legal basis for each purpose;
- legitimate interests pursued, where legitimate interests are the basis;
- recipients or categories of recipients;
- intended transfers outside the EEA and the relevant safeguards or applicable derogation;
- retention period, or criteria used to determine it;
- rights to access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, and portability where applicable;
- right to withdraw consent at any time where consent is the basis;
- right to complain to a supervisory authority;
- whether providing data is a statutory or contractual requirement, whether the person must provide it, and consequences of not providing it;
- existence of automated decision-making or profiling, including meaningful information about the logic and envisaged consequences, where applicable.

If data comes from another source, art. 14 adds source and timing requirements. A notice should distinguish collection from the visitor, server-generated technical data, third-party vendors, and data received through a contact channel. Use purpose-specific legal bases rather than claiming every operation is consent-based.

**Sources:** [GDPR arts. 5, 12-14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj).

## Analytics, storage, cookies, and consent

ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC art. 5(3) requires the subscriber or user to receive clear and comprehensive information and give consent before information is stored in, or accessed from, terminal equipment, unless the storage/access is strictly necessary to provide a service explicitly requested by the user or solely to carry a communication. The Directive is implemented through Member State law, so check the country-specific rule and regulator position.

The strictly-necessary exception is narrow. Storage for authentication, a user-requested feature, or consent preferences may qualify depending on facts. Storage or access useful mainly to the operator, including optional analytics or advertising, should not be treated as necessary merely because it improves the site.

Analytics can also involve GDPR personal-data processing. GDPR art. 6 requires a lawful basis; art. 7 sets consent conditions; art. 5(2) and art. 24 require accountability. Gate scripts, pixels, SDKs, network calls, third-party fonts, and embeds where their operation is consent-dependent, not only cookies. Record what was presented, purpose selected, time, and consent version sufficiently to demonstrate consent, while minimizing the record.

EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 explain that consent requires a clear affirmative act, must be granular and informed, cannot be bundled for multiple unrelated purposes, and must be as easy to withdraw as to give. Rejecting optional processing must not require a materially harder path than accepting it.

**Sources:** [ePrivacy Directive art. 5(3)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2002/58/oj); [GDPR arts. 4(11), 5(2), 6, 7, 24](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj); [EDPB Guidelines 05/2020](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-052020-consent-under-regulation-2016679_en).

## Withdrawal, rights, and retention

Provide a persistent privacy/cookie-settings route. GDPR art. 7(3) requires withdrawal at any time and says it must be as easy as giving consent. On withdrawal, stop future optional collection/use, prevent reactivation, and delete or expire optional client identifiers where feasible. Explain that processing before withdrawal may remain lawful.

The notice should explain how to exercise arts. 15-22 rights. Requests generally must be answered without undue delay and in any event within one month, subject to the regulation's extensions and identity/security limits (art. 12(3)). Objection to processing based on legitimate interests is especially relevant to analytics or direct marketing (art. 21). Erasure is not absolute; exceptions include legal obligations and establishment, exercise, or defence of legal claims (art. 17).

GDPR art. 5(1)(e) requires keeping identifiable data no longer than necessary for its purpose, subject to specified exceptions. State concrete periods where possible: for example, short security-log retention, contact-message retention until the inquiry is resolved plus a justified claims period, and consent records only as long as needed to demonstrate compliance. Vendor and hosting retention must be verified rather than guessed.

**Sources:** [GDPR arts. 5(1)(e), 7(3), 12(3), 15-22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj).

## Controller identity, processors, and transfers

Name the person or legal entity deciding purposes and means. Give a usable postal or electronic contact address. Hosting, analytics, font, CDN, email, and security providers may be processors or independent controllers depending on their role; document the qualification and contract obligations under GDPR arts. 28-29 where a processor is used.

For transfers to a third country, GDPR Chapter V requires an adequacy decision or an appropriate safeguard such as standard contractual clauses, unless a narrow derogation applies. The notice should identify relevant countries or transfer mechanism and explain safeguards. Do not claim “EU hosting” without checking vendor support requests, telemetry, subprocessors, logs, and CDN routing.

**Sources:** [GDPR arts. 4(7), 13(1)(a)-(e), 28, 44-49](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj).

## Are Terms of Service required?

No general GDPR or EU rule requires Terms of Service for a purely informational portfolio that does not offer a transaction, account, user-generated content, or other contractual service. GDPR requires privacy information about processing, not a separate Terms document.

That conclusion changes if the site offers paid work, accepts an offer or booking, distributes software under terms, collects submissions, creates accounts, or otherwise forms contracts. Consumer-contract and distance-selling information duties can then apply. Separately, the e-Commerce Directive requires certain provider-identification information for information-society services, including name, geographic address, contact details, and registration/VAT details where applicable; Member State rules may add requirements.

For this portfolio, publish a privacy notice regardless of whether Terms are published. Add short Terms only if there is a real contractual or content-licensing need; do not use Terms as a substitute for cookie consent or GDPR notice.

**Sources:** [e-Commerce Directive 2000/31/EC arts. 2(b), 5, 6, 10](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/31/oj); [GDPR arts. 12-14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj). The “no universal Terms requirement” conclusion is scope-based: the cited instruments impose targeted information/contract duties, not a blanket Terms-document duty for every informational website.

## Site-specific follow-up

1. Confirm controller name, address, contact channel, establishment, target Member States, and whether a DPO is legally required.
2. Verify production requests and retention for Vercel Analytics, Speed Insights, Microsoft Clarity, Google Fonts, CDN/WAF, hosting logs, and all browser storage.
3. Classify each technology under the applicable Member State ePrivacy implementation; block optional tags and requests before consent.
4. Select and document one lawful basis per processing purpose, transfer mechanism, retention period, and processor/controller role.
5. Publish privacy notice and a persistent withdrawal/settings mechanism. Reassess Terms only if the portfolio begins forming contracts or accepting regulated submissions.
